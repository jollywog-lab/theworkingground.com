Step 3

Build your network

Three tools for thinking about who's in your corner, what life stage you're navigating, and how to build relationships without it feeling transactional.

Your personal board of advisors is the small group of people who support your development in different ways. Each seat serves a distinct function — having all five is useful, but most people start with gaps. Name who's currently in each seat, and who you want there.

Seat 1
Mentor

Someone further along a path you're interested in. Offers experience, perspective, and honest guidance. Ideally in a formal mentoring relationship.

Seat 2
Braintrust

Peers — people at a similar stage — who you think alongside. They challenge your thinking, share what they're learning, and tell you when your reasoning is off.

Seat 3
Sponsor

Someone with influence who advocates for you in rooms you're not in. Different from a mentor — they use their capital on your behalf. The hardest seat to fill, and the most valuable.

Seat 4
Coach

A professional coach — trained to help you think through decisions, build self-awareness, and work through blockers. More relevant from around 30+ or when navigating a significant transition.

Seat 5
Therapist

A mental health professional. Not everyone needs this, but if emotional patterns are getting in the way of decisions or relationships at work, it's the right support — not a mentor or coach's job to fill. Mentoring is not therapy.

Looking at your board

Which seats are empty or thin? Which relationship do you most need to build or deepen in the next 12 months?

Different life stages bring different career questions. Pick the decade that most closely matches where you are now — or the one you're heading into. These are generalisations, not predictions. Use whatever is useful; ignore what isn't.

25–30: Building the foundation

This decade is about acquiring skills, discovering what you're good at, and starting to figure out what kind of work feels meaningful. It's also the decade where most people first realise that effort alone doesn't determine outcomes — and that relationships and visibility matter.

What tends to matter most

Picking up hard skills quickly and being willing to do the less glamorous work
Finding a manager or mentor who invests in your development
Building your braintrust — people who will be your peers for decades
Getting enough breadth to know what you actually want to specialise in
Learning to manage upward, not just downward
What am I learning in this role — and is it still teaching me?
Who in my field do I want to be in 10 years? Have I spoken to them?
Am I being challenged, or just busy?
What do people consistently come to me for?

30–35: The first fork

This is often when the first major career decision comes — whether to go deep in a specialisation, move into management, start something, or make a pivot. The decisions compound. It's also when the personal and professional start to feel like they're in direct competition with each other.

What tends to matter most

Being deliberate about whether you want to be a manager or an individual contributor — the paths diverge here
Identifying and building a sponsor relationship — someone who advocates for you in decisions you're not part of
Getting clear on what you're optimising for: money, impact, flexibility, prestige, meaning — few people can have all of them at once
Protecting relationships and health — both are easy to neglect during peak career-building
Am I moving toward something, or optimising within the current track?
Is this the right organisation for where I want to go, or do I need to leave to get there?
What am I tolerating that I shouldn't be?
Who knows what I'm capable of — and is there someone who doesn't, who should?

35–40: Depth and direction

By this stage, most people have enough track record to be known for something — or realise they want to change what they're known for. If you're in a senior role, you're managing people and navigating politics in ways that require skills beyond technical competence. If you're changing direction, you have credibility to transfer and a clearer sense of what you're trading.

What tends to matter most

Reputation management — being intentional about what you're known for and who knows it
Building capability in others, not just yourself
Deciding which relationships to invest in deeply, rather than maintaining a wide, shallow network
Figuring out whether the trade-offs you've been making are still the right ones
What do I want to be doing in 10 years, and is the current path taking me there?
Am I developing the people around me as well as I'm developing myself?
What would I do if I weren't afraid of being wrong?

40–45: Consolidation and recalibration

This decade often brings a re-evaluation — not necessarily a crisis, but a recognition that the ambitions and trade-offs of your 30s may no longer be the right ones. What you want from work may have shifted. The relationship between identity and career becomes more conscious.

What tends to matter most

Separating identity from job title — which gets harder, not easier, at this stage
Finding work that has meaning beyond advancement — contribution, legacy, craft
Managing energy, not just time — what depletes you vs. what restores you
Giving back in the form of mentoring — which also forces you to clarify what you actually believe
What's my definition of success now — and how has it changed?
Am I staying somewhere out of inertia, or is there a real reason?
What would I regret not having tried?

45–50: The longer game

Career decisions at this stage are often about the next 20 years, not the next 2. Some people are at the peak of their influence; others are actively designing a different kind of life. Both are legitimate. The question is what you want to do with the expertise and relationships you've accumulated.

What tends to matter most

Clarity on what you want the rest of your working life to look like — more of the same, or a deliberate shift
Building toward a portfolio of activities rather than a single job
The quality and depth of your relationships — who you want to keep working with
Deciding how much of your time you want to give to the next generation vs. to your own work
What do I want to have contributed by the time I'm done?
What do I know that I haven't shared yet?
What would I tell my 30-year-old self?
These stages are a rough guide, not a map. Life doesn't follow the same arc for everyone — different starting conditions, different industries, different personal circumstances. Use what fits your situation.

The problem with networking

Most people dislike networking because the transactional version of it is genuinely unpleasant — going to events, collecting business cards, following up with people you don't remember. The version that actually works doesn't look like that.

Strong networks are built through genuine curiosity and consistent contribution over time, not through targeted outreach when you need something. The practical implication: start before you need it.

What actually builds a network

Do interesting work and talk about it. The easiest way to attract people worth knowing is to have a point of view and share it — in writing, in talks, in conversations. You don't need to be famous. You need to be findable.

Be genuinely useful to people before you need anything. Share relevant information. Make introductions between people who should know each other. Remember what people are working on and ask about it. These are not tactics — they're just how good relationships work.

Follow up on conversations that were worth having. If you had a good conversation with someone, send a note within 24 hours. Not a form letter — a specific reference to what you talked about. "I looked up the thing you mentioned about X — here's what I found" is more memorable than "Great to meet you."

Maintain relationships at low effort. You don't need to have coffee with everyone twice a year. A message when you read something relevant, a reaction to a post, a congratulations on something public — these cost nothing and signal that you're paying attention.

Reaching out to people you don't know

Cold outreach works when it is specific, brief, and clear about what you're asking for. The most common mistake is being vague — "I'd love to pick your brain" — or asking for too much from someone with no reason to help you.

What works: a short message (four sentences maximum) that explains who you are, why you're reaching out to this specific person, what you're asking for (a 20-minute call, one question by email, a specific introduction), and makes it easy to say yes or no.

"Hi [Name], I'm [who you are in one sentence]. I've been following your work on [specific thing] and found [specific piece] particularly useful for [specific reason]. I'm currently [relevant context]. Would you be open to a 20-minute call to share your perspective on [specific question]? Happy to work around your schedule."

The response rate is not the measure of success. The quality of the conversations you do have is.

A few things that don't work

Asking for a job in an outreach message. It makes the recipient responsible for your outcome and puts them in an awkward position. Ask for a conversation, not a favour.

Only reaching out when you need something. People notice. Build the relationship before you need it — not during.

Networking at scale instead of depth. Fifty shallow connections are less useful than five real ones. Prioritise the people you can genuinely help and who can genuinely help you.

Treating every senior person as a target. The most useful relationships are often with peers — people at a similar stage who will be your colleagues, clients, and collaborators for decades.
